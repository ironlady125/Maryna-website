A guide from a UFC fighter

Water-loading weight cut

I competed in two UFC weight classes and know the cut from the inside. Here’s how water loading works, what fight week looks like day by day, and — most importantly — how to rehydrate without harming your health.

This is educational material about how professional athletes cut weight. A cut puts serious stress on the heart, kidneys and brain. Only do it under the supervision of a doctor, coach or sports nutrition specialist. Weight cutting is not appropriate for children, teenagers or people with heart or kidney conditions.

1. What a weight cut is and why fighters do it

In MMA, fighters usually weigh in the day before the fight. In the remaining ~24–30 hours, part of the lost water can be regained. That’s why many athletes temporarily reduce body weight through water and gut content, make weight, and then rehydrate.

A cut has two parts:

  • Chronic weight loss — weeks and months of proper nutrition during camp (fat, excess weight).
  • Acute cut — the last 5–7 days: water, sodium, carbs, fiber.

2. The golden rule: lose most of it early

The more you cut in the final week, the worse you’ll feel in the fight and the higher the health risk.

8–10 weeks out

Walking weight no more than 8–10% above the division limit.

A week before weigh-in

Within ~5–6% of the division limit.

Water cut

Ideally no more than 3–5% of body mass. Anything beyond that — only with medical supervision.

Example: 125 lb (57 kg)

Camp weight ~134–137 lb (61–62 kg), a week out ~132 lb (60 kg).

3. How water loading works

If you drink a very large amount of water for several days in a row, your body adapts to the excess: production of water-retaining hormones (antidiuretic hormone, aldosterone) drops and the kidneys flush fluid actively.

When you then sharply reduce intake, the body keeps flushing water for a while out of inertia. As a result, weight drops faster and with less discomfort than simply restricting fluids.

Research supports this approach: for example, in a study by Australian researchers (Reale et al., 2018), athletes who drank about 100 ml of water per kg of body weight for three days and then sharply reduced intake lost more weight than a control group, with no adverse effects observed.

4. Five levers of an acute cut

1) Water

Load with a large volume, then cut intake sharply 24–30 hours before weigh-in.

2) Sodium (salt)

Keep salt normal during loading (this helps avoid diluting electrolytes). Reduce sodium 1–2 days before weigh-in: less sodium — less water retained.

3) Carbohydrates (glycogen)

Each gram of glycogen in muscle and liver holds about 3 g of water. Lowering carbs 3–5 days out reduces glycogen stores and drops another 1–2% of body mass. After weigh-in, carbs are the top priority.

4) Fiber (gut content)

A low-residue diet 2–4 days out reduces the weight of gut content — up to another 1–1.5% of body mass without any dehydration. Fewer vegetables, whole grains and legumes; more white fish, eggs and white rice.

5) Active sweating

Hot baths, sauna, training in a sweat suit — used only at the very end and only if the weight hasn’t come off. This is the hardest and riskiest stage.

  • Weigh yourself every morning and evening on the same scale and log it.

6. The final hours: if the weight isn’t there

If you’re slightly over the night before, controlled sweating is used:

  • Hot bath (often with Epsom salt) — short 10–15 minute bouts, then 5–10 minutes wrapped in towels, weighing between bouts.
  • Sauna — short sessions, never alone.
  • Light work in warm clothes — shadowboxing, stationary bike.

Stop immediately if you feel dizzy or confused, have a racing heart, cramps or nausea, stop sweating or start slurring words. No fight is worth your health.

7. Rehydration after weigh-in

Rehydration is half of the cut. Done right, you step into the cage strong and fresh.

  1. First 30–60 minutes: an oral rehydration solution (water + sodium + a little glucose) in small portions, roughly 250–500 ml every 15–20 minutes. Don’t chug liters at once.
  2. Daily target: drink about 1.25–1.5 times the fluid you lost (e.g. after a 3 kg cut — around 4–4.5 L before the fight).
  3. Carbohydrates: light, familiar food — rice, pasta, potatoes, white bread, honey, peeled fruit. Aim for 5–10 g of carbs per kg over the day.
  4. Sodium: salt your food and use electrolytes — sodium helps you hold on to the water you drink.
  5. Avoid: fatty or spicy food, lots of fiber and anything new you haven’t eaten before.
  6. Fight morning: a normal light breakfast, keep drinking, check your weight — by fight time you should have regained most of what you lost.

8. Dangers and bans

  • Diuretics and laxatives are forbidden. Diuretics are on anti-doping prohibited lists and are extremely dangerous for the heart.
  • IV rehydration after weigh-in is banned in the UFC under anti-doping rules — rehydrate by drinking and eating.
  • Don’t cut too much. Extreme cuts reduce strength, endurance and reaction speed, and severe dehydration leaves the brain less protected from strikes.
  • If cutting is always brutal — your division might be higher. I changed weight classes myself and won after that.
