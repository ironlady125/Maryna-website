A guide from a UFC fighter

How an MMA fighter prepares

Everything I learned over my years in the UFC: the types of training, what makes MMA unique, and how to build a fight camp so you step into the cage in the best shape of your life.

1. What makes MMA the hardest sport

Mixed martial arts isn’t one sport — it’s several at once. A fighter must strike with hands and feet, wrestle standing and against the fence, attack and defend on the ground — all for 3 five-minute rounds (or 5 rounds in main events and title fights) with one minute of rest.

That shapes how fighters prepare:

  • Many skills at once. Striking, wrestling, jiu-jitsu and, most importantly, the transitions between them.
  • Mixed energy systems. A fight is explosive bursts (a takedown, a flurry) on top of continuous work. You need both an aerobic base and anaerobic power.
  • Contact and injuries. Training load has to be balanced so you keep progressing and still make it to the fight healthy.
  • Weight class. You need to be as strong as possible at your weight and make weight correctly (see the separate guide).
  • The mind. In the cage you’re alone. Mental preparation is training like everything else.

2. Types of MMA training

Striking: boxing, kickboxing, Muay Thai

My foundation is boxing and kickboxing, and I’m convinced: good striking wins fights. Striking sessions include:

  • Shadowboxing — sharpening technique, footwork, rhythm and defense without a partner.
  • Pad work — the coach simulates the opponent while you drill combinations and counters.
  • Heavy bag — power and endurance, 3–5 minute rounds.
  • Partner drills — defense, slips, ducks, counters, checking leg kicks.
  • Clinch — knees and elbows, head control, breaking the clinch.
MMA specifics

The MMA stance is lower and wider than in boxing because you must be ready to sprawl. Strikes are often combined with the threat of a takedown, and takedowns are disguised with strikes.

Wrestling: freestyle, Greco-Roman, cage wrestling

  • Takedowns — single and double legs, clinch throws, trips.
  • Takedown defense (sprawl) — for a striker it’s a matter of survival.
  • Fence wrestling — unique to MMA: controlling the opponent, switching positions, getting up along the cage.
  • Get-ups — returning to your feet quickly.

Jiu-jitsu, grappling and submissions

My UFC debut ended with an armbar in 90 seconds — the ground wins fights too. Ground training includes:

  • Positional control — mount, side control, back, guard.
  • Joint locks and chokes — armbar, triangle, arm-triangle, rear-naked choke, guillotine.
  • Defense and escapes — mount escapes, choke defense, sweeps.
  • Positional sparring — starting from a specific position with a specific task.

MMA-specific work: putting it all together

Separate skills are worthless without connections. MMA sessions drill:

  • transitions from strikes to takedowns and back;
  • ground and pound — striking from top and defending from bottom;
  • cage work, owning the center of the octagon, cutting off angles;
  • tactical scenarios: “opponent is a wrestler”, “opponent is a tall striker”, and so on.

3. Sparring the right way

Technical

Light contact, focus on technique and timing. Can be done often.

Situational

A limited task: striking only, fence wrestling only, ground only.

Full MMA sparring

Close to a fight, with headgear and gloves. Once a week in camp, no more.

Opponent-specific

A partner copies your next opponent’s style — stance and favorite combinations.

Important

Hard sparring is the main source of injuries and concussions. Your last hard sparring session should be 10–14 days before the fight; after that, only technique and speed.

4. Strength & conditioning (S&C)

Strength training

A fighter’s goal isn’t to build big muscles but to become stronger and more explosive at their weight.

  • Maximal strength: squats, deadlifts, presses, pull-ups — 3–5 sets of 3–5 reps.
  • Explosive power: plyometrics (jumps), med-ball throws, cleans, sled pushes.
  • Core strength: anti-rotation exercises, planks, rope and sandbag work.
  • Neck and grip — a must for fighters: they protect against knockouts and help in grappling.

Conditioning (energy systems)

  • Aerobic base: running, cycling, swimming for 30–60 minutes at an easy heart rate. It lets you recover faster between rounds and bursts.
  • Anaerobic intervals: 20–40 seconds all-out / rest — mimicking the bursts of a fight.
  • Round-based work: 5 minutes on / 1 minute off — exactly like a fight, switching exercises within the round.
Iron Lady tip

Fight conditioning is built in the gym with a partner, not just on a treadmill. The best cardio for a fighter is hard rounds on the pads, in wrestling and on the ground.

A professional camp usually lasts 8–12 weeks. Load is built in waves so you peak on fight day.

7. Sample workouts

Striking session (60 minutes)

  1. Warm-up (10 min): jump rope, joint mobility, light shadowboxing.
  2. Technique (15 min): one combination, e.g. “jab — cross — low kick”, with defense afterwards.
  3. Pads (4 × 3 min): combinations + counters on the coach’s signal.
  4. Bag (4 × 3 min): minute one technique, minute two power, minute three pace.
  5. Finisher (5 min): 20 seconds fast straight punches / 10 seconds rest.
  6. Cool-down (5 min): stretching, breathing.

“Fight-like” conditioning (3 × 5-minute rounds)

Each round: 1 min fast shadowboxing, 1 min sprawl burpees, 1 min ground and pound on a bag on the floor, 1 min wall get-ups, 1 min sled pushes or med-ball slams. 1 minute rest between rounds.

8. Recovery

  • 8–10 hours of sleep — the main recovery “supplement”; nothing works without it.
  • Active recovery: swimming, easy cycling, yoga and mobility.
  • Massage and physio — injury prevention and work on tight muscles.
  • Load monitoring: track resting heart rate, how you feel and sleep quality. If everything gets worse — reduce volume.

9. A fighter’s nutrition

  • Protein: 1.6–2.2 g per kg of body weight per day for muscle recovery.
  • Carbohydrates: the main fuel for intense training; amount depends on the day’s training volume.
  • Fats: fish, nuts, olive oil — for hormonal health.
  • Water: drink regularly through the day; urine should be pale.
  • Weight: keep your walking weight not far above your division so the cut stays safe.

10. Mental preparation

  • Visualization: run the fight in your head every day — the walkout, the first seconds, the hard moments and your victory.
  • Breathing: slow-breathing techniques help control adrenaline in the locker room and between rounds.
  • Scenarios: decide in advance what you’ll do if you get hit hard or end up on your back.
  • Trust your preparation: confidence comes from the work you’ve done. When the camp was honest, there’s nothing to fear.

11. Common mistakes

  • Too much hard sparring and too little technique.
  • Training only your strengths and avoiding weaknesses.
  • Neglecting sleep and recovery.
  • Walking around far above your division and then “killing yourself” with the cut.
  • Copying a pro’s program without accounting for your level.
