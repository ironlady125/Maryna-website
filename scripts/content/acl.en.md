> **Important**
>
> This content is for information only. Timelines and exercises depend on your graft type, associated injuries (such as a meniscus repair) and your surgeon’s recommendations. Always agree on your program with your doctor and physiotherapist. Progress by **criteria**, not by the calendar.

## 1. What the ACL is and why it tears

The **ACL (anterior cruciate ligament)** stabilizes the knee by stopping the shin from sliding forward and rotating. In combat sports it’s most often injured during a sharp pivot on a planted foot, an awkward landing, in wrestling, a takedown or a leg lock.

Typical signs: a pop at the moment of injury, rapid swelling within hours, and a feeling of instability — the knee “gives way”. The diagnosis is made by a doctor based on examination and MRI.

## 2. Can you recover at home?

Yes — most rehab can be done at home if:

- you have a clear phase-based program;
- a physiotherapist regularly checks your technique and progress (including online via video);
- you respect progression criteria and don’t rush;
- in later stages you have access to a gym or at least resistance bands, a step and dumbbells.

The online format works well for monitoring range of motion, exercise technique, gait and load progression. In-person visits are still needed for your surgeon’s check-ups and for equipment-based strength testing where available.

## 3. Before surgery: “prehab”

If surgery is planned, 2–6 weeks of preparation make recovery noticeably easier. Goals:

- reduce swelling and restore **full knee extension**;
- restore flexion and a normal gait;
- switch the quadriceps back on — a straight leg raise without the knee sagging;
- maintain strength in the other leg, core and upper body.

After consulting their doctor, some patients recover **without surgery**, through a long-term exercise program. This decision is individual.

## 4. Phase 1 (weeks 0–2): protection and full extension

**Goals:** reduce swelling and pain, restore full extension, activate the quads, walk safely.

- **Swelling control —** Ice 15–20 min several times a day, compression, leg elevated above heart level.
- **Extension —** Heel propped on a roll with the knee hanging free — 5–10 minutes several times a day.
- **Quad sets —** Tighten the thigh, pressing the knee down — 10 × 10 seconds.
- **Straight leg raise —** Only if the knee doesn’t sag when lifting. 3 × 10.
- **Flexion —** Heel slides along the floor or wall — gradually up to 90°.
- **Ankle pumps —** Every hour, for circulation.

> **Criteria to progress**
>
> - Full extension (matching the healthy leg)
> - Flexion ≥ 90°
> - Straight leg raise without lag
> - Swelling decreasing, walking with minimal support — when cleared by your doctor

## 5. Phase 2 (≈ weeks 2–6): normal gait and a strength base

**Goals:** flexion to 120°+, walking without crutches or a limp, closed-chain strengthening (foot planted).

- **Mini squats** and box squats to a chair — 3 × 12–15;
- **Wall sits** — 3–5 × 20–45 sec;
- **Glute bridge** on two legs, then one — 3 × 12;
- **Step-ups** onto a low step — 3 × 10;
- **Calf raises** — 3 × 15;
- **Single-leg balance** — 3 × 30 sec, then eyes closed or on a cushion;
- **Stationary bike** with no resistance — 10–20 min, as soon as flexion allows;
- **Banded lateral walks** — for the glute medius.

> **Caution**
>
> With a hamstring graft, resisted knee flexion is usually restricted for the first weeks. With a meniscus repair, squat depth and loading restrictions are stricter. Check with your surgeon.

> **Criteria to progress**
>
> - Near-full range of motion
> - Normal gait without a limp
> - 30-second single-leg stance with good control
> - No increase in swelling after exercise

## 6. Phase 3 (≈ weeks 6–12): strength

**Goals:** systematically build quad, hamstring and glute strength, and knee control in single-leg exercises.

- **Bulgarian split squats** and lunges — 3–4 × 8–10;
- **Single-leg Romanian deadlift** with a dumbbell — 3 × 10;
- **Controlled step-downs** (knee not caving in) — 3 × 10;
- **Weighted squats** (dumbbell, backpack), gradually deeper;
- **Banded knee extensions** — within the range your physio allows;
- **Hamstring curls** with a band or on a stability ball;
- **Cardio:** stationary bike, elliptical, swimming without breaststroke kick.

Increase load gradually: if the knee is more swollen or painful the next day, take a step back.

> **Criteria to start running (usually around 12 weeks)**
>
> - No swelling or pain when walking and after training
> - Quad strength ≥ 70–80% of the healthy leg
> - 10+ single-leg squats with good knee control
> - Clearance from your surgeon / physio

## 7. Phase 4 (≈ months 3–6): running, jumping, agility

- **Running progression:** walk–run intervals (e.g. 1 min run / 1 min walk), gradually increasing running time;
- **Plyometrics:** two-leg jumps in place first, then forward and sideways, then single-leg;
- **Landing technique:** soft, knee over the foot, no caving inward;
- **Agility:** ladder drills, changes of direction at 45° first, then 90°;
- **Strength work continues** 2–3 times a week — strength remains the top priority.

## 8. Phase 5 (≈ months 6–9+): return to sport

Return happens in steps: non-contact sport drills → partner drills without resistance → light contact → full training → competition.

| Criterion                                                 | Target                           |
|-----------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------|
| Quadriceps and hamstring strength                         | ≥ 90% of the healthy leg         |
| Single-leg hop tests (distance, triple, crossover, timed) | ≥ 90% of the healthy leg         |
| Swelling, pain, instability                               | None                             |
| Psychological readiness                                   | No fear of loading the knee      |
| Time since surgery                                        | Usually no earlier than 9 months |

Research shows that returning too early significantly increases re-injury risk: in a well-known Norwegian study (Grindem et al., 2016), the risk of knee re-injury fell by roughly half for each month that return to sport was delayed, up to 9 months after surgery.

> **For MMA fighters**
>
> **Wrestling and ground sparring** come back last: shots, twisting and leg locks put the most dangerous loads on the knee. First striking and shadowboxing, then wrestling with a non-resisting partner, then positional sparring, and only then full sparring.

## 9. A sample week at home (phases 2–3)

| Day       | Workout                                                      |
|-----------|--------------------------------------------------------------|
| Monday    | Leg strength (squats, bridges, step-ups) + balance           |
| Tuesday   | Stationary bike 20–30 min + mobility                         |
| Wednesday | Leg strength (lunges, Romanian deadlifts, step-downs) + core |
| Thursday  | Upper body + balance                                         |
| Friday    | Leg strength + glute band work                               |
| Saturday  | Low-intensity cardio, a walk                                 |
| Sunday    | Rest, swelling control                                       |

Every day: extension and flexion exercises until full range of motion is restored.

## 10. Home equipment

- Resistance bands and mini-bands of different strengths
- A step platform or sturdy step
- A chair, towel and foam roll
- A pair of dumbbells or a weighted backpack
- A balance cushion (optional)
- A cold pack
- A phone with a tripod — for video sessions and technique checks

## 11. When to see a doctor urgently

- Fever, redness or discharge from the wound — possible infection;
- Calf pain, swelling and redness — possible blood clot;
- A sudden increase in swelling, or the knee “locking”;
- New episodes of the knee giving way;
- Sharp pain that doesn’t ease at rest.
